TL;DR — One printed book is the text of record: the 1910 Scribner volume in which Roosevelt's publisher collected the addresses of his European tour. Every quotation here was checked against it, and every link below returned a live page on 6 September 2026 bar one, noted in place. Every entry says what it settles — because several famous stories about this passage rest on sources that establish far less than they are asked to.

A bibliography that only lists links is a display of effort. The useful part is the assessment: this scan proves the wording, that biography proves his whereabouts, that film proves nothing about 1995.

1. The text itself

Four kinds of witness exist online and they do not agree in every word. The differences are small, but the version most people copy is not the one Roosevelt's publisher printed.

2. Roosevelt: life and papers

The archives answer questions of fact; the biographies answer questions of judgement.

  • Theodore Roosevelt Center Digital Library — Dickinson State University · Establishes free digitised correspondence and ephemera from the Library of Congress, Harvard and the Park Service, with provenance on every record. Not completeness; a selection of a vast paper trail.
  • The Theodore Roosevelt Papers — Library of Congress, Manuscript Division · Establishes the primary collection most Roosevelt scholarship rests on. Not anything checked here: the Library refused automated requests, so the link is listed unconfirmed. It resolves in a browser.
  • Sagamore Hill National Historic Site — National Park Service · Establishes the Oyster Bay house as kept, and the Service's account of the post-presidential years. Not textual detail; interpretation, not an edition.
  • Theodore Roosevelt Birthplace — National Park Service · Establishes a reliable outline of the early life. Not anything about 1910; even the house is a 1920s reconstruction.
  • Theodore Roosevelt: life in brief and essays — Miller Center, University of Virginia · Establishes a signed scholarly overview of the presidency the speech assumes. Not 1909–12, which falls between two administrations.
  • Theodore Roosevelt Association — founded 1919, congressionally chartered · Establishes a journal and pointers to collections. Not disinterest: a memorial body, admiring by design.
  • Lawrence F. Abbott, Impressions of Theodore Roosevelt (1919) — Internet Archive · Establishes a first-hand account of the tour by the man who accompanied Roosevelt and edited the 1910 volume. Not impartiality: a friend, writing after Roosevelt's death.
  • Edmund Morris, The Rise of Theodore Roosevelt (1979) — Open Library catalogue record (the publisher's own page for this title 404s) · Establishes the standard narrative life to 1901. Not the speech; it ends nine years early.
  • Edmund Morris, Theodore Rex — publisher's page · Establishes the presidency, 1901–09 — the record he was defending at the Sorbonne. Not the tour, which begins after it ends.
  • Edmund Morris, Colonel Roosevelt — publisher's page · Establishes 1909–19: Africa, the tour, the break with Taft, the Bull Moose run — the volume covering this speech. Not a text; trace its quotations to the printing.
  • Kathleen Dalton, Theodore Roosevelt: A Strenuous Life — publisher's page · Establishes a scholarly life cooler than Morris on Roosevelt's self-mythology — a corrective when the passage starts sounding like autobiography. Not a competing text.

3. The neighbouring speeches

Roosevelt had argued this case for a decade, and had already tried its mirror image — a defence of criticism — before a hostile press.

  • The Strenuous Life: Essays and Addresses (1900) — Project Gutenberg 58821 · Establishes a lifetime printing of the Chicago address of 10 April 1899. Not the delivery; the printed version is the prepared one.
  • “The Strenuous Life” — English Wikisource · Establishes a convenient text headed with the right occasion, the Hamilton Club, Chicago, 10 April 1899. Not its provenance: the page carries an unsourced tag.
  • “The Man with the Muck Rake” — English Wikisource · Establishes the 1906 address, where Roosevelt attacks reckless exposure journalism while insisting honest exposure is indispensable — the distinction the arena paragraph is used to erase. Not the date: the header leaves the year blank.
  • Nobel lecture, Christiania, 5 May 1910 — The Nobel Foundation · Establishes an authoritative text for the address given twelve days after the Sorbonne, on the same tour. Not a link to the arena passage; the subject is peace machinery.

The Cairo, Berlin, Guildhall and Oxford addresses of 1910 — including the Romanes Lecture, “Biological Analogies in History” — are all in the Scribner volume linked at the top of this page, and show how much was recycled and how much written for Paris.

4. The afterlife

  • Richard Nixon, address announcing his resignation, 8 August 1974 — The American Presidency Project, UC Santa Barbara · Establishes the best-documented borrowing: the transcript carries the passage, Roosevelt named — shortened and garbled, not the 1910 wording. Not where Nixon got his text.
  • Brené Brown, Daring Greatly (2012) — Open Library catalogue record (the publisher's own page for this title 404s) · Establishes the book that carried the passage to a new readership, titled from its last clause. Not what the book argues; see this map's page.
  • Brené Brown, “The power of vulnerability” — TEDxHouston, recorded 6 June 2010 · Establishes the date and reach of the talk that preceded the book. Not the Roosevelt connection: the arena framing arrives two years later, with the book.
  • John Carlin, Playing the Enemy: Nelson Mandela and the Game That Made a Nation (2008) — publisher's page · Establishes the book the film came from, by a journalist who interviewed the principals. Not the Roosevelt story attached to it, examined on the Mandela–Pienaar page.
  • Invictus (2009), dir. Clint Eastwood — Wikidata catalogue record · Establishes the film's identity, year and director. Not history: the text it puts in Mandela's hands is Henley's poem, not Roosevelt's paragraph — a distinction that collapses in retellings.
  • W. E. Henley, “Invictus” (1888) — Poetry Foundation · Establishes the poem's text, so the two passages can be told apart. Not anything about Roosevelt, who has no connection to it whatever.

5. Context for 1910

  • “France” — 1914–1918-online, International Encyclopedia of the First World War (peer-reviewed) · Establishes the Third Republic around 1910: unstable ministries, the anticlerical settlement, a falling birth rate — why Roosevelt's remarks on family size landed so pointedly. Not anything specific to the lecture.
  • Histoire de la Sorbonne — Chancellerie des Universités de Paris, custodian of the building · Establishes the nineteenth-century rebuilding that produced the Grand Amphithéâtre — the hall Abbott describes as ringed with statues of Pascal, Descartes and Lavoisier. Not capacity, or who was admitted.
  • Le Figaro, 1910, day by day — Gallica, Bibliothèque nationale de France · Establishes that the Paris press of that week is digitised and free to read, the issue of 24 April 1910 included. Not what those pages say: the full-text endpoint sits behind an anti-robot check, and this map did not extract the coverage.
  • “President Roosevelt to lecture at the Sorbonne” — press release from The Outlook, Theodore Roosevelt Center · Establishes that the Paris and Oxford invitations were announced in advance, together. Not the arrangements on the day.
  • “The sermon at the Sorbonne” — contemporary article, Sagamore Hill collection via the Theodore Roosevelt Center · Establishes that the lecture drew criticism as well as applause. Not a representative view; it is one clipping.

6. Where the record is thin

Four claims travel with this passage everywhere; none could be settled from the sources above. All four are marked unsettled wherever they appear here.

Mandela handing the passage to François Pienaar in 1995. Told constantly, sourced almost never; the film that fixed it in memory shows Henley's poem, not Roosevelt. What the participants said is on the dedicated page.

“Roosevelt carried a copy in his pocket.” No such document appears in any archival record located here. The archive shows the reverse: he wrote it, circulated copies in December 1908, and delivered it sixteen months later.

Attendance at the Sorbonne. The figure repeated everywhere traces to one line in Abbott's introduction, where he writes the hall held “probably” three thousand people. He was there — and he was Roosevelt's friend and editor, and it is a guess, not a count.

The French press reaction. Abbott quotes a correspondent claiming Le Temps printed fifty-seven thousand copies for French schoolteachers — a second-hand figure inside a partisan memoir. The Paris papers are digitised and public; nobody, this map included, appears to have checked.

7. How this map was built

One text was chosen as canonical and everything measured against it: the 1910 Scribner printing of African and European Addresses, published within months of the speech by Roosevelt's own house, edited by a man who was in the room. Every Roosevelt quotation here was checked word for word against it, and where the circulating online text differs the difference is shown rather than smoothed away. Every external link was fetched on 6 September 2026 and returned a live page, bar one: the Library of Congress collection page refused automated requests and is listed unconfirmed. Claims that could not be traced to a primary or strongly-sourced secondary account are labelled unsettled wherever they appear. One expected link was dropped rather than printed: the long-cited Almanac of Theodore Roosevelt at theodore-roosevelt.com now serves an unrelated site.
