Adjacent ideas: Stoics, skin in the game, the strenuous life

“Citizenship in a Republic” · Sorbonne, 23 April 1910 · page 12 of 13
Nearest relativeTheodore Roosevelt, The Strenuous Life (1899–1900), Project Gutenberg ↗
TL;DR — The arena paragraph is not an original idea, and most of its relatives state it more precisely than Roosevelt does. The Stoics separate effort from outcome and then counsel indifference to the outcome, which Roosevelt refuses. Taleb replaces “are you trying?” with a question that can actually be checked: do you bear the downside? Roosevelt’s own 1899 speech says the same thing more bluntly and drags an empire in with it. What the 1910 passage has that none of the others do is a picture.

Reading the arena passage next to its neighbours does two things at once. It shows how much of the paragraph is inherited — the contest metaphor, the contempt for spectators, the priority of effort over result are all far older than Roosevelt. And it shows where the paragraph is weakest, because the neighbours make the same argument with criteria attached, and the arena passage does not.

The Stoic dichotomy of control

The oldest relative is the opening of Epictetus’ Enchiridion, the handbook of his teaching compiled by his student Arrian in the early second century. It begins with a partition:

“There are things which are within our power, and there are things which are beyond our power. Within our power are opinion, aim, desire, aversion, and, in one word, whatever affairs are our own. Beyond our power are body, property, reputation, office, and, in one word, whatever are not properly our own affairs.”— Epictetus, Enchiridion I, trans. Thomas Wentworth Higginson (Liberal Arts Press, 1948; Project Gutenberg)

Set that beside the middle of Roosevelt’s paragraph and the overlap is exact. Roosevelt credits the man “who strives valiantly; who errs, and comes short again and again, because there is no effort without error and shortcoming.” Striving is up to you. Coming short is not. The whole rhetorical trick of the passage — that it can praise a man who loses — depends on having already made Epictetus’ cut between the act and the result.

Marcus Aurelius, writing to himself a generation later, uses the same cut as a work instruction. Meditations II.5 tells him to think “every moment… as a Roman and a man to do what thou hast in hand with perfect and simple dignity, and feeling of affection, and freedom, and justice; and to give thyself relief from all other thoughts.” The thing in hand; nothing else.

Then the two traditions split, and they split hard. Epictetus does not merely say the outcome is not up to you; he says you should stop caring about it. Enchiridion XVII gives the image of the actor: “this is your business—to act well the given part, but to choose it belongs to another.” The good Stoic is content with the poor man’s role. Roosevelt is not content. He wants his man to win. Read the sentence the posters leave out: “Well for these men if they succeed; well also, though not so well, if they fail, given only that they have nobly ventured, and have put forth all their heart and strength.” Though not so well. Four words that make Roosevelt a bad Stoic. He grades on the outcome; he just refuses to grade only on it.

That difference matters. Stoic indifference is a technique for surviving defeat; the arena passage is a technique for accepting risk. One is consolation, the other is recruitment. People who reach for the passage after losing something are borrowing the Stoic use of it, not Roosevelt’s.

Amor fati and the wrestling-ground

Marcus also supplies the closest thing in antiquity to Roosevelt’s arena image, and it is an athletic one:

“The art of life is more like the wrestler’s art than the dancer’s, in respect of this, that it should stand ready and firm to meet onsets which are sudden and unexpected.”— Marcus Aurelius, Meditations VII.61, trans. George Long (1862); text from Standard Ebooks

A note on the numbering, because it is a genuine trap. Long’s edition is unnumbered in most modern reprints, and the seventeenth-century Meric Casaubon translation — the one Project Gutenberg carries as ebook 2680 — divides Book VII differently and prints this passage as VII.33. The standard modern reference is VII.61, where it sits in the Loeb edition: C. R. Haines renders it “the business of life is more akin to wrestling than dancing, for it requires of us to stand ready and unshakable against every assault however unforeseen.” Cite the translation, because the numbers do not agree.

Four sections earlier, at VII.57, Marcus writes: “Love that only which happens to thee and is spun with the thread of thy destiny.” That is the sentiment usually labelled amor fati — though the Latin tag is Nietzsche’s, coined in The Joyful Wisdom §276 (“Amor fati: let that henceforth be my love!”), not Marcus’s. Marcus has the thought without the phrase.

The wrestling image and the arena image are cousins, and the family resemblance hides the important difference: Marcus’s arena is inside his own head. He is the emperor. There is no one he needs to beat, and no crowd whose opinion he is contesting; the onsets he is bracing for are illness, betrayal, irritation, the deaths of people he loves. Roosevelt’s arena is public by construction. It has spectators, it has a result, and the whole point of the passage is the relation between the man on the sand and the men in the seats. Strip the audience out of Roosevelt and the paragraph collapses — there is no one left to be a critic.

Seneca on the spectator

Seneca gets nearer to Roosevelt than either of them, because Seneca is writing about a literal arena. Moral Letters 7 is an attack on crowds, and it turns into a description of an afternoon at the games:

“In the morning they throw men to the lions and the bears; at noon, they throw them to the spectators… And what of it? Granted that, as a murderer, he deserved this punishment, what crime have you committed, poor fellow, that you should deserve to sit and see this show?”— Seneca, Epistulae Morales 7.4–5, trans. Richard M. Gummere (Loeb, 1917); text at Wikisource

Seneca reports what the crowd shouts — “Why does he meet the sword in so cowardly a way? Why does he strike so feebly? Why doesn’t he die game?” — and his verdict is that watching does moral damage to the watcher. That is Roosevelt’s cold and timid souls, eighteen centuries early, in the seats of an actual amphitheatre. The parallel is thematic, not genealogical: there is no evidence Roosevelt had this letter in mind, and Seneca’s complaint is about cruelty and idleness rather than criticism. He is not defending the man on the sand; he is worried about the man in the stands.

A second Senecan passage inverts Roosevelt entirely. In De Providentia 2.9 the spectator is not contemptible — he is God: “Behold a sight worthy to be viewed by a god interested in his own work, behold a pair worthy of a god, a brave man matched with evil fortune, especially if he himself has given the challenge” (trans. Aubrey Stewart, 1889). Same arena, same brave man, opposite judgement on the audience.

Skin in the game

The sharpest modern relative is Nassim Nicholas Taleb’s Skin in the Game: Hidden Asymmetries in Daily Life (Random House, 2018), and it is a stronger argument than Roosevelt’s for one specific reason.

Taleb’s subject is the asymmetry between people who bear the consequences of their opinions and people who do not. A surgeon who operates on you carries a share of your risk; the pundit who recommends a war, the analyst who recommends a stock, the consultant who recommends a reorganisation carry none of it. His claim is not that these people are cowards. It is that their information is unreliable, because nothing in their environment punishes them for being wrong — and a system that lets unfiltered opinion accumulate at the top becomes fragile in ways nobody inside it can see.

Put the two tests side by side. Roosevelt’s is effort: is this person striving, spending himself, daring greatly? Taleb’s is exposure: if this goes wrong, does it go wrong for them? The second is better because it can be checked. Effort is invisible and self-reported; everyone believes they are trying. Exposure is a fact about the world — look at the contract, the capital, the reputation, the body, and establish whether the person loses anything by being wrong. That converts a mood into a criterion.

It also fixes the passage’s worst failure mode. The arena quotation is routinely used by powerful people to dismiss criticism — see the critiques page — and effort-as-criterion cannot stop them, because they are unquestionably working hard. Exposure-as-criterion can. An executive whose failed decision costs other people their jobs and costs him a bonus is not in the arena in Taleb’s sense; he is in the stands with a good view. Roosevelt has no way to say that. Taleb does.

What Taleb loses is the thing Roosevelt has. “Skin in the game” is a criterion. It is not an image, and nobody has ever wept at it.

Roosevelt’s own “The Strenuous Life”, 1899

Eleven years before the Sorbonne, on 10 April 1899, Roosevelt gave a speech to the Hamilton Club in Chicago that is the arena paragraph’s direct ancestor and states the conviction without any of the 1910 speech’s balance:

“It is hard to fail, but it is worse never to have tried to succeed.”— Theodore Roosevelt, “The Strenuous Life”, Hamilton Club, Chicago, 10 April 1899 (Project Gutenberg)

The thesis sentence runs: “I wish to preach, not the doctrine of ignoble ease, but the doctrine of the strenuous life, the life of toil and effort, of labor and strife.” Everything in the arena paragraph is present here in rougher form.

What changed by 1910 is the subject. The 1899 speech is about national vigour: addressed to a Republican club four months after the Treaty of Paris, it argues that the United States must not shrink from the territories it has just acquired. “We can not avoid the responsibilities that confront us in Hawaii, Cuba, Porto Rico, and the Philippines,” Roosevelt says, and the doctrine of effort is deployed to make refusal look like cowardice. It gets uglier: “It is cowardly to shrink from solving them in the proper way; for solved they must be, if not by us, then by some stronger and more manful race.” He describes the Filipino dead as “wild and ignorant foes” and blames the war’s casualties on domestic critics — “the prattlers who sit at home in peace.” That is the arena argument used exactly as its critics say it gets used: to shut down opposition to a policy by attacking the character of the opponents.

The 1910 speech is about the ordinary citizen’s character. Its subject, in Roosevelt’s own words at the Sorbonne, is “individual citizenship”, and its claim is that “the average citizen must be a good citizen if our republics are to succeed.” The arena paragraph there is about a life, not a foreign policy. But the two speeches share a doctrine, and it should be said plainly: the strenuous-life idea was born attached to an imperial argument, and the 1899 text is where anyone who wants to separate the two has to do the work.

Nietzsche’s neighbourhood, carefully

Nietzsche is always dragged into this company, usually via one line from Twilight of the Idols: “From the military school of life.—That which does not kill me, makes me stronger” (“Maxims and Arrows” §8, trans. Anthony M. Ludovici, 1911). The surface resemblance is real. Both men affirm struggle; both despise the safe and the tepid.

The difference underneath is total, and one sentence from the Sorbonne makes it visible: “The success of republics like yours and like ours means the glory, and our failure the despair, of mankind.” Roosevelt’s arena exists inside a republic. The reason to enter it is duty — to fellow citizens, to a form of government he believes is on trial. Effort is good because self-government requires it. Nietzsche is making no civic argument whatever; his objection to the tepid is aesthetic and physiological, not a complaint about a bad citizen. The two get shelved together because both are quotable about hardship, which is not a philosophical relationship.

The modern versions, and how good the evidence is

Three contemporary ideas rhyme with the passage, and it is worth being blunt about how much each one has been tested.

  • Growth mindset (Carol Dweck, Mindset, 2006) — the closest psychological cousin, because it is a theory about what error means. The replication record is mixed. A 2018 pair of meta-analyses in Psychological Science found the correlation between mindset and academic achievement very weak and intervention effects small and inconsistent; a large national randomised trial in Nature in 2019 found a real but small effect, concentrated in lower-achieving students. Honest summary: a modest effect in some populations, not the transformative one the popular version promises.
  • Grit (Angela Duckworth, Grit, 2016) — sustained passion and perseverance toward long-term goals. A 2017 meta-analysis in the Journal of Personality and Social Psychology concluded that grit predicts performance only weakly and is largely redundant with conscientiousness. The construct may be real; the claim that it is a distinct and unusually powerful predictor is not well supported.
  • Antifragility (Taleb, Antifragile, 2012) — the property of systems that improve under stress rather than merely surviving it. A conceptual proposal about risk, not an empirical psychology, and it should be judged as such.

None of this makes the arena passage wrong. It does mean the research literature people invoke to back it up is thinner than the invocation implies.

What the passage has that the others do not

Every idea on this page is more precise than the arena paragraph. Epictetus draws a cleaner line, Taleb supplies a test that can actually be run, Marcus is more useful at three in the morning. And yet none of them is tattooed on anybody, and the arena passage is.

The reason is not that Roosevelt was the better thinker. He was not. It is that he wrote a picture and they wrote categories. “The dichotomy of control” is a filing label: it has to be taught, and it is retained as a proposition. “Whose face is marred by dust and sweat and blood” is a face. It arrives already understood, survives translation and paraphrase, and can be recalled under stress by someone who has never read a page of philosophy. Three nouns, no abstraction anywhere.

That is the passage’s real advantage over its better-argued neighbours, and also the source of its danger. A criterion tells you when it does not apply; a picture never does — which is why the arena quotation gets borrowed with equal conviction by someone risking everything and by someone risking nothing. Keep Roosevelt’s picture for what pictures are good at, which is getting a person to enter. Borrow Taleb’s question for what criteria are good at: working out whether they are actually in there.

Carry this away: the arena passage is the weakest argument in its own family and the strongest image. Use the image to start; use “who bears the downside?” to check.
