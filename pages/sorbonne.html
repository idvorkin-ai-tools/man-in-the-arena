23 April 1910: the day, the hall, the audience

“Citizenship in a Republic” · Sorbonne, 23 April 1910 · page 02 of 13
Eyewitness accountLawrence F. Abbott, introduction to African and European Addresses (Scribner, 1910) ↗
TL;DR — A private citizen weeks out of Africa lectured the University of Paris in its largest hall on a Saturday afternoon, in English, to the whole French cabinet and several hundred hand-picked students. The paragraph the world now quotes did draw applause on the day — a man who was there says so. But the papers led with property and with the birth rate, and almost every crisp number attached to the occasion comes from someone estimating, not counting.

The arena paragraph is usually met without its room. Knowing the room changes it: not a rally and not a locker room but a university lecture, given by a man holding no office, to a republic in the middle of an election, at the invitation of an institution that expected a scholar and got a preacher. This page rebuilds the occasion from what 1910 left behind, and marks where the record thins into repetition.

Who invited him, and why he was in Europe at all

Roosevelt had not planned to come. He left the presidency in March 1909 for a Smithsonian collecting expedition in British East Africa, meaning to sail home the way he had come. What changed it, by his own account, was an invitation from Lord Curzon, Chancellor of Oxford, to give the Romanes Lecture. Once he accepted that, everything else accreted.

“Immediately afterwards, I received and accepted invitations to speak at the Sorbonne in Paris, and at the University of Berlin… In Paris, after consultation with the French Ambassador, M. Jusserand, through whom the invitation was tendered, I decided to speak more generally, as the citizen of one republic addressing the citizens of another republic.”— Theodore Roosevelt, foreword to African and European Addresses, Sagamore Hill, 15 July 1910

Two things follow. The invitation came from the University of Paris but travelled through Jean Jules Jusserand, France's ambassador in Washington for the whole of Roosevelt's presidency — a personal line, not a chancery one. And the register was chosen in advance: Berlin and Oxford would get scholarship, Paris citizenship. The one address on the tour written about how ordinary people should behave is the one that produced the arena.

The University's side survives too. Louis Liard, vice-rector of the Académie de Paris, introduced him; his remarks are in the Revue internationale de l'enseignement. He thanks Roosevelt for accepting « l'austère intermède d'une conférence à ses étudiants », and ends on a line English retellings give as “you unite morality with politics, and right with might”:

« Vous unissez la morale à la politique et le droit à la force. Vous êtes un rude soldat et un pacifique, un penseur et un homme d'action, un prédicateur des fortes vertus et un exemple vivant des vertus que vous prêchez. »— Louis Liard, introducing Roosevelt, Revue internationale de l'enseignement, vol. 59 (1910), p. 385

The hall

The Grand Amphithéâtre was twenty-one years old that spring, inaugurated on 5 August 1889 as the centrepiece of the Third Republic's rebuilt university: a hemicycle under a dome, wrapped in two levels of galleries. Behind the podium hangs Puvis de Chavannes's Le Bois sacré, twenty-five metres of pale allegory in which Literature, Science and the Arts gather around the Sorbonne. Six niches hold statues — Robert de Sorbon, Richelieu, Descartes, Pascal, Lavoisier, Rollin. Abbott, who was in the room, describes exactly that: “statues of the great masters of French intellectual life—Pascal, Descartes, Lavoisier, and others”, one of “the Puvis de Chavannes's most beautiful mural paintings”, French and American flags behind the speaker.

The setting matters because the speech opens by using it: “Strange and impressive associations rise in the mind of a man from the New World who speaks before this august body in this ancient institution of learning.” He is describing the room to the people sitting in it.

The day, the room, and the trouble with the numbers

He gave the university the whole day. Wire copy filed from Paris that afternoon has him at the Institut de France at one o'clock, taking his seat as a foreign member of the Académie des sciences morales et politiques, where its president Émile Boutroux welcomed him and he replied in French. The AP dispatch in the Calumet News puts the lecture at three in the Grand Amphitheater and reports him most cordially received; the Montgomery Advertiser's version adds a flag-hung facade and a street crowd nobody tried to hold back.

On composition the reports agree: the Springfield Weekly Republican of 28 April says the audience was all of the members of the French cabinet, nine hundred students selected from the University of Paris, and many distinguished guests. Note selected — a delegation, not a crowd that turned up. On size, the record is a mess.

Those cannot all describe the same room on the same afternoon. A century of refitting separates the modern seat count from the 1910 estimates, and a hall packed with people standing in aisles and galleries holds far more than one with numbered seats. Honest summary: several hundred students, the government, the academic establishment, and as many more as could be got in. Any page giving a precise total is repeating a guess.

How he delivered it

In English. Abbott records that Roosevelt “occasionally with quizzical humor… interjected an extemporaneous sentence in French”, and gave one passage twice — first in English, then in his own French. That passage was the one about property, and its provenance is startling:

“A passage of peculiar interest was the statement of his creed regarding the relation of property-rights to human rights; it was not in his original manuscript but was written on the morning of the lecture as the result of a discussion of the subject of vested interests with one or two distinguished French publicists.”— Lawrence F. Abbott, introduction to African and European Addresses, 1910

The rest had been drafted in the winter of 1909, before he left the White House. So the line the newspapers led with was about four hours old when he said it, and the paragraph the world now quotes was over a year old.

No source found here gives the running time. At about 8,800 words an unhurried pace puts it near an hour, longer with applause and the French repetitions — but that is arithmetic, not evidence.

What the papers actually reported

Here the record is unusually clear, because American wire coverage is digitised and searchable. On the day and after, the arena passage was not the story; two other things were. The first was property. Washington's Evening Star ran “Humanity First, Roosevelt Tells French Savants” over a deck explaining that rights are identical until they conflict, and then property belongs to man and not man to property. The Springfield Weekly Republican headed its account “Humanity Before Property”. That is the paragraph written that morning.

The second was the birth rate. The San Francisco Call gave it the front page on 24 April under “France Warned of Race Suicide by Roosevelt”; the Montgomery Advertiser ran “Have Plenty of Children”. Abbott confirms it from inside the hall: Roosevelt attacked race suicide “when speaking to a race whose population is diminishing, and was loudly applauded.” That is paragraph 15, the part of the speech that has aged worst; see the other nine-tenths.

The arena was not invisible. The Salt Lake Tribune of 24 April subheaded the wire story, through OCR damage, “Says the Critic Is Not Essential but Strong Brave Man Is” — the one contemporary headline found here reaching for the passage. And Abbott is unambiguous:

“the passage ending with a reference to ‘cold and timid souls who know neither victory nor defeat,’ was delivered with real eloquence, and aroused a long-continued storm of applause.”— Lawrence F. Abbott, introduction to African and European Addresses, 1910

So the familiar claim that nobody noticed the paragraph until decades later is wrong. A search of the Library of Congress newspaper archive for the exact phrase “cold and timid souls” returns American pages within days — the Redwood Gazette of 27 April 1910, Atlanta's Golden Age on 28 April, and on through May. What is true is that the passage was one striking thing among several, not yet the whole speech.

Why those passages landed in that country, in that month

France in April 1910 was not a neutral audience for a lecture about revolution, class hatred and the family. Four things were live in the room.

  • An election, immediately. The first round of the legislative elections fell on 24 April — the next day; the second on 8 May. Roosevelt lectured citizens on their duties the afternoon before they voted.
  • Anticlericalism at full boil. The 1905 law separating the churches and the state was five years old and still bitterly contested, not least inside the university. A speech insisting that religious and anti-religious appeals are equally disqualifying in a candidate — paragraph 32 — was not abstract.
  • Syndicalist unrest. Troops had killed strikers at Villeneuve-Saint-Georges in 1908, and Aristide Briand, prime minister that afternoon, would break the October railway strike by conscripting the railwaymen. Roosevelt's refusal of both “the doctrinaires of an extreme individualism” and those of “an extreme socialism” in paragraph 24 landed inside that fight. Abbott prints a Paris correspondent's claim that the government's firmness against the May Day demonstration a week later was a repercussion of the visit — which the correspondent himself calls “an extraordinary verdict”, and which nothing else supports.
  • The birth rate. Depopulation was a national obsession with an organised lobby: Jacques Bertillon's Alliance nationale pour l'accroissement de la population française had campaigned since 1896. An American telling Paris that wilful sterility is a crime walked into an argument the French had been having with themselves for forty years. Hence cheers rather than offence.

Where it sat in the tour

Four weeks earlier, on 28 March 1910, he had told the National University in Cairo that character precedes constitutions: “You can no more make a man really educated by giving him a certain curriculum of studies than you can make a people fit for self-government by giving it a paper constitution.” Paris is that argument turned on an audience which already had self-government — the average citizen, not the paper, holds a republic up. Twelve days later came the Nobel lecture at Christiania (5 May), then the Romanes Lecture at Oxford (7 June). All are in the same 1910 volume; Paris is the only one addressed to citizens as citizens.

Documented, and merely repeated

Documented by a source consulted here: the date, the hour and the hall; the invitation from the University of Paris tendered through Jusserand; Liard's introduction, in print; the French cabinet and roughly nine hundred selected students; delivery in English with French interjections and the property passage repeated in French; that passage written the morning of the lecture; sustained applause at the “cold and timid souls” line and at the birth-rate passage; American headlines leading on property and race suicide.

Contemporary but soft: every crowd number, for the reasons in the table above.

Repeated but unverified here: the much-quoted “nine hundred students and an additional two thousand ticket-holders”, and the ministers-in-court-dress detail, are attributed to Edmund Morris's Colonel Roosevelt (2010) by the online essays carrying them; Morris's text was not consulted, so treat the phrasing as second-hand. That Le Temps printed fifty-seven thousand copies and sent one to every schoolteacher in France appears in Abbott's book only inside a private letter from an unnamed American officer — plausible, one anonymous witness. Claims of a Hachette vellum edition and a pocket edition selling five thousand copies in five days have no checkable source.

What to carry away is not a number. The room applauded the arena passage on the day, and then the papers filed on something else — and the line they filed on had been written that morning. A speech is not one paragraph until later people decide it is.
