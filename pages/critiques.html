Critiques and misreadings

“Citizenship in a Republic” · Sorbonne, 23 April 1910 · page 11 of 13
Canonical textAfrican and European Addresses, Scribner 1910 ↗
TL;DR — The paragraph survives some of these objections and not others. Against the charges that it glorifies failure and that it silences critics, the speech around it answers, and the answer is in the text. Against the charges that entry is unevenly distributed, that the vocabulary is built out of a specific ideal of manliness, and that the famous users are the people it worked out for, it has no defence. What is left is narrower than the poster and more useful.

The paragraph is quoted as if it were self-evident. It is not, and lifted out of its speech it acquires failure modes its author did not design. This page states six objections at full strength, checks each against the text, and reports which stand.

1. It glorifies failure

The objection: in ordinary use the passage has become a way of not counting outcomes. A venture collapses and the quotation appears — on a slide, in a farewell post — converting the loss into a credential. Trying becomes the achievement, which destroys the feedback that makes effort worth anything: if failing while daring greatly earns the standing of succeeding, nobody need find out why the thing failed. The passage does not merely tolerate that reading; it supplies the vocabulary, and the vocabulary is flattering.

What the text says: Roosevelt ranks them, in a sentence most quoters never reach.

“Well for these men if they succeed; well also, though not so well, if they fail, given only that they have nobly ventured, and have put forth all their heart and strength.”— Theodore Roosevelt, Citizenship in a Republic, Sorbonne, 23 April 1910

Though not so well. Success is better — a flat contradiction of the equivalence the popular use asserts. Given only that is a condition, not a courtesy: the failure earns its standing only if the venture was noble and the effort total. Two tests a lazy failure fails.

What survives: the answer lives entirely in the part nobody quotes, which is itself a fact about the passage. As excerpted it does function as an excuse, and excerpting is how it lives in the world. Even read whole it gives no way to tell a noble failure from an avoidable one — “there is no effort without error and shortcoming” is true, and is also what a person says who has stopped examining their errors.

2. It is a shield against accountability

The objection: the most consequential modern use is by powerful people deflecting scrutiny. The executive whose product harmed users, the official whose decision cost lives — each can answer examination with a quotation that reclassifies the examiner as a coward. If the arena confers immunity then immunity scales with power, since power is what puts a person in arenas: the more consequential the decision, the less answerable the decider. That is backwards, and the passage performs the inversion in a voice that sounds like courage.

It is also a category error about how societies check power. Auditors, judges, regulators, coroners, peer reviewers and reporters are all structurally outside what they examine, and that outsideness is the source of their value. Applied literally, the test abolishes every institution built to be independent.

What the text says: two paragraphs later Roosevelt states the opposite.

“But if a man's efficiency is not guided and regulated by a moral sense, then the more efficient he is the worse he is, the more dangerous to the body politic. … If the man works for evil, then the more successful he is the more he should be despised and condemned by all upright and far-seeing men.”— Theodore Roosevelt, Citizenship in a Republic, paragraph 21

Scrutiny intensifying as capability rises is the precise opposite of a shield. And the target is narrower than it looks: in paragraph 9 the man attacked confines himself “to criticism of the way others do what they themselves dare not even attempt” — a posture, not a function, as the page on what he meant by “the critic” works through.

What survives: the misuse is not accidental, and the text is partly to blame. The paragraph attacks critics as a type without naming the criticism it wants preserved, so a reader who has only the paragraph cannot tell the cynic from the auditor. Against the excerpt the objection stands.

3. The arena is not equally open

The objection: the metaphor treats entry as a decision — a person is in the arena or in the seats, and which one is a matter of nerve. That describes a world in which a public failure costs everyone about the same, and no such world exists. The capacity to dare greatly is purchased: with capital that makes a failed venture an anecdote rather than an eviction; with credentials that keep the second attempt fundable; with a visa that does not expire when the contract does; with health, and insurance not tied to a job; with nobody whose rent depends on this month's income. A parent supporting a household is not choosing the seats but pricing risk correctly.

The sharp end is the moral label the passage supplies for those who do not enter: “those cold and timid souls who know neither victory nor defeat.” That reads constraint as character. Because the label is moral rather than economic, it aims contempt at the people with the least slack while flattering those whose slack made their venture survivable.

What the text says: nothing. The speech rests on the premise that character is what matters — “above mind and above body stands character.” That the capacity to exercise it is unevenly distributed is never addressed. Roosevelt concedes that resources gate participation, insisting a man “must pull his own weight first” before his surplus strength is of public use, but never turns that on the arena.

What survives: the objection stands, undefended. The one softening is that the passage never specifies the arena's size; read at the scale of a hard conversation the entry cost falls sharply and the point holds. That is a rescue by reinterpretation, not a defence of what was written.

4. The gender is not incidental

The objection: the vocabulary is not a stylistic accident of 1910 that a modern reader can silently translate. It is the argument. “The man in the arena”, “the strong man”, the men “who quell the storm and ride the thunder” — the virtue praised is a specific ideal of manliness, preached for a decade under that name. Swapping in “person” does not neutralise a text whose author assigns men and women different work in the same speech.

What the text says: both things. Roosevelt opens by addressing citizenship to both — success or failure turns on how “the average man, the average woman, does his or her duty”. But when he specifies the duty, the spheres separate:

“The homely virtues of the household, the ordinary workaday virtues which make the woman a good housewife and house-mother, which make the man a hard worker, a good husband and father, a good soldier at need, stand at the bottom of character.”— Theodore Roosevelt, Citizenship in a Republic, paragraph 22

Housewife on one side; worker, father, soldier on the other — and the birth-rate passage hardens the split further. The arena falls on the man's side of that line, which is also the side the credit is on.

Two things complicate this. Roosevelt's position moved: two years later the Progressive Party platform he ran on pledged “to the task of securing equal suffrage to men and women alike”, a plank no major party had carried. And the passage was in practice universalised — Brené Brown took her title from it and read the arena as any room entered at risk of shame, reaching an audience Roosevelt was not addressing (the page on that book traces what that costs).

What survives: modernising the pronoun makes the passage portable, and portability is why it is still alive. What it loses is the ability to see what the passage was for — Roosevelt's arena is continuous with the soldier, the frontier and the strenuous life, and his contempt for the fastidious and the cloistered is contempt for insufficiently manly men. Translate that away and the sentence gets gentler and less honest.

5. We only hear from the survivors

The objection: the evidence for the arena thesis comes from a population the thesis has already filtered. The people who quote it in public dared greatly and came out the other side — the founder whose second company worked, the athlete who lost a final and won the next. Those whose venture destroyed them do not give commencement addresses; their sample is invisible by construction.

The error has a name: survivorship bias, reasoning about a process from the cases that cleared a filter. Abraham Wald's wartime work for the Statistical Research Group is the canonical illustration: given data on where returning bombers were hit, he said to armour the places with no holes, because aircraft hit there never came back to be measured.

It is unusually strong here. The passage is a claim about the value of risk, and risk is precisely a process that removes its losers from the sample. Its most famous users are winners quoting it about a loss they survived: enough failure to make the words feel earned, enough success to be handed a microphone. Each citation is evidence only about people for whom the loss was survivable.

What survives: more than expected, because Roosevelt makes no claim about outcomes. He says the failure is worth less than the success; he never says the venture will probably pay. A rule for allocating esteem is not refuted by a bad base rate. The objection lands on how the passage is used and glances off what it says.

6. Roosevelt did not always live it

The objection: the author was a professional critic. Four years before the Sorbonne, as a sitting president, he attacked investigative journalists in the “Man with the Muck-Rake” address — allowing they were “often indispensable to the well-being of society; but only if they know when to stop raking the muck”, and coining a lasting slur for reporters doing work he had not done. In 1899 he dismissed opponents of the Philippine war as “the prattlers who sit at home in peace”: disqualification, not argument.

The record is worse than sneering. That same 1899 address held of the Filipinos that “Many of their people are utterly unfit for self-government, and show no signs of becoming fit.” In November 1906 he ordered 167 Black soldiers of the 25th Infantry discharged without honour after a shooting in Brownsville, Texas — no court-martial, no hearings, no identified shooter, Medal of Honor recipients among them, the order held back until the midterms had closed. He defended it publicly in the language of mutiny, murder and treason; the Army reversed it in 1972.

What the text says: Roosevelt saw the objection coming and stated it against himself.

“Moreover, the preacher of ideals must remember how sorry and contemptible is the figure which he will cut, how great the damage that he will do, if he does not himself, in his own life, strive measurably to realize the ideals that he preaches for others.”— Theodore Roosevelt, Citizenship in a Republic, paragraph 23

What survives: hypocrisy is not evidence that the thesis is false — that inference is a plain ad hominem. It is evidence of something else: the thesis is easy to hold, hard to apply, and fails in the direction its author failed, which is outward, at other people. Brownsville is the counter-example it most needs to answer: a man deep in the arena was wrong, the critic outside it was right, and the correction took sixty-six years. That is not a reason to discard the paragraph, but a reason never to be the one deciding whose criticism counts.

The anti-intellectual misreading

A smaller misuse, but common: the passage as a case against thinking — stop analysing, get in the arena. The setting alone should stop it. The speech was given at the Sorbonne, to an assembled university, by a man who published some three dozen books — and the paragraph immediately after the arena passage compliments French intellectual life, praising “a high artistic and literary development is compatible with notable leadership in arms and statecraft”. Two paragraphs later: “I pay all homage to intellect, and to elaborate and specialized training of the intellect.”

What Roosevelt attacks is a posture, not learning — refinement grown into “a fastidiousness that unfits him for doing the rough work of a workaday world”, and the man who would act “if only the conditions of life were not what they actually are”. The same speech warns against the opposite failure, the “so-called ‘practical’ men” whose practicality is contempt for standards.

What survives

Grant all six: the excerpt excuses failure, it has been weaponised against legitimate scrutiny, entry is bought rather than chosen, the vocabulary is gendered to its foundations, the witnesses are self-selected, and the author violated it at scale.

Two things remain. The first is a claim about esteem: the person who attempts something and comes short deserves more standing than the person who attempted nothing and is therefore never wrong — not equal standing to the one who succeeded, and not unconditionally, but more than nothing. The second costs something to hold: the safety of never being in a position to be judged is not a virtue, and a life organised around never being publicly wrong is a life spent in the seats. Neither licenses ignoring critics. Both are demands made of the reader. Pointed outward, the paragraph becomes the thing it was written against.

The test that keeps the passage honest: it is used correctly when it costs the user something — when it makes them enter, or makes them listen to a critic. It is used incorrectly the instant it becomes a reply.
