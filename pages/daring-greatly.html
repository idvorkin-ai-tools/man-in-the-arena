Brené Brown and Daring Greatly

“Citizenship in a Republic” · Sorbonne, 23 April 1910 · page 09 of 13
The bookDaring Greatly: How the Courage to Be Vulnerable Transforms the Way We Live, Love, Parent, and Lead ↗
TL;DR — Daring Greatly (Gotham Books, 2012) takes its title from six words buried in the middle of Roosevelt’s arena sentence. Brené Brown is a research professor at the University of Houston who spent a decade doing qualitative research on shame; she uses the passage as the organising image of a book about vulnerability, and in the process quietly changes what the arena is. Roosevelt’s arena is public, competitive and owed to a republic. Brown’s is any room you enter without a guarantee. Both readings are defensible. They are not the same reading, and the version most people now carry is hers.

The previous page lists a century of borrowings. This one takes the largest seriously, because for most readers alive today the transmission runs backwards: they meet Roosevelt through Brown, not Brown through Roosevelt. That makes the delta worth stating precisely — not to score points against a bestseller, and not to pretend the two are saying the same thing.

Who she is, and what she was studying

Brené Brown is a research professor at the University of Houston Graduate College of Social Work, where she holds the Huffington Foundation Endowed Chair. Her curriculum vitae, hosted by the university, records a PhD in social work from Houston in 2002 and a research professorship there since 2004; since 2020 she has also been a visiting professor of management at the University of Texas at Austin’s McCombs School of Business.

The shape of the work before the fame matters here. Brown is a qualitative researcher — someone who codes interview transcripts rather than running experiments. Her 2006 article in Families in Society was titled “Shame resilience theory: A grounded theory of women and shame”; her first book, self-published in 2004, was Women and Shame. By the time the arena paragraph entered her vocabulary she already had a decade of coded interviews about what shame does to people, and a technical name for what helps.

The public turn came in 2010. Her CV lists a TEDxHouston talk given on 5 June 2010 under the title “On Being Enough”; TED published it that December as “The power of vulnerability”, and it has since been viewed more than seventy million times on TED’s own site. That talk contains no Roosevelt at all.

How the passage reached her

The Roosevelt connection arrives afterwards, and she says so on stage. At TED in March 2012 — the talk published as “Listening to Shame” — she introduces it as something recent and personal:

“There’s a great quote that saved me this past year by Theodore Roosevelt. A lot of people refer to it as the ‘Man in the Arena’ quote.”— Brené Brown, “Listening to Shame”, TED2012 (recorded 2 March 2012)

The year she means is the year after the TEDx talk went viral, when a researcher who had spent her career studying what it feels like to be judged became a person a great many strangers had opinions about. The passage did not arrive as scholarship. It arrived as first aid.

What she recites next is worth noticing, because this map cares about textual fidelity. Her spoken version ran: “It is not the critic who counts… The credit goes to the man in the arena whose face is marred with dust and blood and sweat. But when he’s in the arena, at best, he wins, and at worst, he loses, but when he fails, when he loses, he does so daring greatly.” The 1910 text reads “dust and sweat and blood”, and not “he wins…he loses” but “who at the best knows in the end the triumph of high achievement”. Speakers compress, and this is a talk rather than a citation — but the compression is directional: Roosevelt’s cadence about achievement becomes a binary of winning and losing, and the losing is the half she keeps. The book, six months later, prints the passage at the front; its introduction is called “My Adventure in the Arena”.

What she actually takes from Roosevelt

Two things. The geometry — an inside and an outside, a floor and a set of seats, and a moral asymmetry between them. And permission to fail in public, which is the rare thing this passage grants and the load-bearing beam of a book about showing up without a guarantee. The publisher’s description says nothing is as hurtful as “standing on the outside looking in and wondering what it would be like if we had the courage to step into the arena.” On stage she gave the personal version: “I know it’s seductive to stand outside the arena, because I think I did it my whole life, and think to myself, I’m going to go in there and kick some ass when I’m bulletproof and when I’m perfect.” That is Roosevelt’s cold and timid soul restated as procrastination rather than cowardice.

What she adds that is not in the speech

Roosevelt names courage and leaves the mechanism unexamined. Brown supplies one, and it is not courage. It is exposure. Her glossary for Dare to Lead defines vulnerability as “the emotion that we experience during times of uncertainty, risk, and emotional exposure. It’s having the courage to show up, fully engage, and be seen when you can’t control the outcome.” The preface of Daring Greatly states it as a thesis: “Vulnerability is not weakness… Our only choice is a question of engagement.” The cost of the arena, on this account, is not the dust and the blood. It is being seen while covered in them.

She also names an enemy Roosevelt never mentions. His obstacle is external — the fastidious man, the cynic, the fop, the critic in the seats. Hers is internal, and has a research vocabulary behind it: shame, which her glossary defines as “the intensely painful feeling or experience of believing that we are flawed and therefore unworthy of love, belonging, and connection.” In the 2012 talk the arena door is where that fight happens: “When you walk up to that arena and you put your hand on the door… shame is the gremlin who says…” Roosevelt assumes the reluctance is contemptible. Brown treats it as diagnosable.

The cheap seats and the one-inch square

The third addition is practical, and it is the piece most often quoted without its safety catch. Brown’s term for criticism that costs the critic nothing is “criticism from the cheap seats”. Her Dare to Lead glossary uses the phrase in passing and then supplies a physical exercise for deciding whose opinion you actually owe attention to — a list she calls a square squad:

“To get clear about who belongs on your square squad, use a one-inch by one-inch piece of paper, write down the names of the people in your life whose opinions really matter. The paper is small because it forces you to narrow the list to only those people who have earned the right to an opinion.”— Brené Brown, Dare to Lead glossary of key language, skills, tools and practices (2021)

The safety catch is in the same entry, and it is the part that gets dropped. The square is explicitly not a fan club: these are “the people who care enough about you to be honest, rather than telling you what you want to hear”, who “should not be people who just blindly agree with you no matter what”, and who “will point out when you are out of your integrity or when you have messed up”. A list that cannot tell you that you were wrong is not a square squad. It is an echo.

The rest of her apparatus cuts the same way. She publishes an Engaged Feedback Checklist — ten conditions for being ready to give feedback, including “I’m open to owning my part” — and a glossary entry, “What’s my part?”, which insists that if you are being brave at work “there is a part with your name on it.” Whatever the poster implies, the machinery is built to keep criticism flowing, only through a narrower and better-vetted pipe. Her own version of the payoff is a sentence the official reading guide reprints from the book: “Nothing has transformed my life more than realizing that it’s a waste of time to evaluate my worthiness by weighing the reaction of the people in the stands. The people who love me and will be there regardless of the outcome are within arm’s reach.”

An arena that is indoors, and open

Roosevelt’s examples are public and large: the man who bears the brunt of the day, the men who quell the storm and ride the thunder. Brown’s are domestic and small. Her reading guide asks about marriage, parenting, feedback conversations, the emotions readers numb, the reactions they fear from their own families. The arena in her hands is a first date, a pitch meeting, an apology. That is a relocation rather than a dilution: she keeps the moral structure and moves it into rooms with furniture.

The other change is who is admitted. Roosevelt’s passage says “the man” repeatedly, and the surrounding address is explicit that its subject is the male citizen of a republic; the critiques page takes up how much of that is incidental. Brown’s corpus starts from the opposite end. Daring Greatly reports that “every successful woman whom I’ve interviewed has talked to me about the sometimes daily struggle to push past ‘the rules’”, and treats men’s shame as a separate, specific problem: “men live under the pressure of one relenting message: Do not be perceived as weak.” The invitation goes to everybody, and it goes first to the people Roosevelt’s sentence does not address.

Where the two readings pull apart

Roosevelt, 1910Brown, 2012 onward
The arena is public and competitive: politics, war, civic life.The arena is relational: any moment of being seen without a guarantee.
The mechanism is character and effort.The mechanism is vulnerability — tolerating exposure.
The obstacle is other people’s cynicism.The obstacle is your own shame.
The point is duty to a republic.The point is connection and wholeheartedness.
Winning is explicitly better than losing.The scoreboard is largely beside the point.
A rebuke aimed at the timid.An invitation offered to the frightened.

The scoreboard line deserves its exact wording, because it is the clearest place the two part company. Roosevelt is not indifferent to outcomes. Later in the same paragraph he ranks them:

“Well for these men if they succeed; well also, though not so well, if they fail, given only that they have nobly ventured, and have put forth all their heart and strength.”— Theodore Roosevelt, Citizenship in a Republic, Sorbonne, 23 April 1910

“Though not so well” is the hinge. Roosevelt is consoling the loser, not congratulating him, and the consolation is explicitly second prize. Any reading that treats failure as its own achievement has to walk past that clause. Brown’s framing does — not by misquoting it, but by asking a different question. She asks what it costs to enter. He asks what the republic needs from the people who do.

The bad-faith version

There is a use of “get in the arena” that belongs to neither of them: the move that converts an argument into a credential check. An objection is raised, and instead of an answer the objector is asked what they have ever built. It is unanswerable by design, since the only qualification it really recognises is agreement with the person invoking it, and it is available to anyone who has ever done anything at all. Used that way the passage stops being about courage and becomes a device for making criticism inadmissible — roughly the opposite of what Roosevelt spends the preceding paragraphs defending.

What the record supports, and what it does not. Brown’s published materials are unambiguous that the people whose judgement you admit must include people who will tell you that you have messed up, and she distributes a feedback framework that presumes criticism is worth having. That is strong evidence against the licence reading. It is not the same as a documented statement from her warning that “get in the arena” is being weaponised, and this page found none it could verify from a primary source. The argument above is made in this map’s own voice, not attributed to her.

The test that keeps it honest is built into the exercise, not into the poster. A one-inch square is small enough to exclude strangers and large enough to hold someone who will tell you that you were wrong. If everyone on your square agrees with you, you have not narrowed the list — you have stacked it.

What came after

Rising Strong (2015) is about the part that happens after the fall, which the 1910 paragraph gestures at and never describes. Dare to Lead (2018) ports the apparatus into management, and is where the square squad and the cheap seats are set down as named tools; both came from Penguin Random House. In 2019 Netflix released Brené Brown: The Call to Courage, a filmed talk. None of this changes the 2012 reading; it repeats it at increasing scale, which is how a paragraph written for a Paris lecture hall becomes the default vocabulary for talking about risk at work.

Which is the honest summary. Brown did not misread Roosevelt. She extended him, in a direction he did not go and probably would not have chosen. The extension is coherent, it is grounded in a real body of interview research, and it has been useful to an enormous number of people. It is also a translation, and translations lose things. What this one loses is the republic.
