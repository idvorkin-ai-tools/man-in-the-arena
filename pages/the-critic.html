What he meant by “the critic”

“Citizenship in a Republic” · Sorbonne, 23 April 1910 · page 07 of 13
Primary sourceRoosevelt, “Sedition, a Free Press, and Personal Rule”, Kansas City Star, 7 May 1918 ↗
TL;DR — The arena paragraph is quoted to shut critics up. The paragraph immediately before it does the opposite: it defines a specific character — the poseur cynic who criticises what he will not attempt — and then ends by naming someone worse, “the man who sneers alike at both criticism and performance.” Roosevelt is condemning contempt for criticism in the same breath. Every one of the twelve times the words critic, cynic or sneer appear in the speech, they appear inside that portrait. Twelve years later, out of office, he wrote that refusing to criticise a president is “morally treasonable to the American public.” His real distinction is between criticism that serves the work and criticism performed instead of work — a sound one, which does not survive being turned into a rule about who may speak.

The arena paragraph opens on a negative: It is not the critic who counts. Read cold, that sentence is a permission slip. It has been used to wave away an auditor, a reviewer, a reporter, a dissenting engineer, a spouse. But the sentence is the eleventh paragraph of a lecture, and it arrives with a running start. Roosevelt has spent the two preceding paragraphs building a portrait of a particular man, and the portrait has a name in it, and the name is not critic. This page reconstructs that portrait from the printed text, checks it against what Roosevelt wrote about criticism in office and out of it, and asks how much of the claim survives.

The paragraph before the paragraph

The audience matters. Roosevelt was addressing the French academic establishment — professors, students, ministers, men of letters — and paragraph 8 turns to face them. Leaders should be drawn from classes like this one, he says, “but only provided that those classes possess the gifts of sympathy with plain people and of devotion to great ideals.” Then the warning: there are “certain failings against which it is especially incumbent that both men of trained and cultivated intellect, and men of inherited wealth and position, should especially guard themselves, because to these failings they are especially liable; and if yielded to, their—your—chances of useful service are at an end.”

That dash — their—your— — is the hinge of the whole passage. He is not describing a class of people somewhere else. He is telling the room that this is their occupational disease. Paragraph 9 is the diagnosis.

“Let the man of learning, the man of lettered leisure, beware of that queer and cheap temptation to pose to himself and to others as the cynic, as the man who has outgrown emotions and beliefs, the man to whom good and evil are as one. The poorest way to face life is to face it with a sneer.”— Theodore Roosevelt, Citizenship in a Republic, ¶ 9

Note the target. It is not an activity — evaluating, objecting, finding fault. It is a pose: a performance of having outgrown caring, worn because it is cheap and looks superior. And the audience for it is doubled — “to himself and to others” — because Roosevelt thinks the cynic is his own first mark. The definition is precise: “the man to whom good and evil are as one.” Someone who cannot tell good work from bad is not a critic but the opposite of one. Criticism requires a standard; the cynic's position is that no standard applies.

Every use of the word, accounted for

The speech runs to roughly 8,800 words across 39 paragraphs. The words critic, criticise, criticism, cynic (and its inflections) and sneer appear twelve times in the whole address. All twelve are in paragraphs 9 and 10. There is no other place in the lecture where Roosevelt discusses criticism at all.

Two things fall out of that list. First, the modifiers are never absent: criticism is condemned only when it is criticism “of the way others do what they themselves dare not even attempt”, or of “work which the critic himself never tries to perform”. Every condemnation carries a qualifying clause, and the clause does the work. Strip it and the sentence stops being his. Second, critic in paragraph 10 arrives already defined by paragraph 9. The famous opening is a pronoun with an antecedent, and the antecedent is the poseur.

The sentence that breaks the usual reading

Paragraph 9 ends on a ranking. Roosevelt has just called the cynic's stance a mask for weakness — men who “seek, in the affectation of contempt for the achievements of others, to hide from others and from themselves their own weakness.” Then:

“The role is easy; there is none easier, save only the role of the man who sneers alike at both criticism and performance.”— Theodore Roosevelt, Citizenship in a Republic, final sentence of ¶ 9

That is the last thing said before “It is not the critic who counts…” It is a superlative: the cynic's role is the easiest there is, except for one easier still — sneering at criticism as well as at performance. Roosevelt ranks contempt for criticism as the lazier posture of the two, puts it in the emphatic final position, and then writes the sentence everybody memorises.

So the quotation, as commonly deployed, does something the text specifically forbids one sentence earlier. Using “It is not the critic who counts” to dismiss criticism because it is criticism is exactly the sneer at criticism that Roosevelt has just called the easiest role in the world.

What the rest of the speech asks the citizen to do

The lecture never returns to the word criticism, but it keeps demanding the activity. Judgement of other people's conduct is one of the duties the speech is built around. Roosevelt says that success is not a defence: “If the man works for evil, then the more successful he is the more he should be despised and condemned by all upright and far-seeing men” (¶ 21). He says a citizenry that declines to make that judgement has disqualified itself — if people “grow to condone wickedness because the wicked man triumphs, they show their inability to understand that in the last analysis free institutions rest upon the character of citizenship”. The section on class hatred (¶ 30) ends on a line that is a mandate for scrutiny of the powerful: “Ruin looks us in the face if we judge a man by his position instead of judging him by his conduct in that position.”

None of that is possible without people standing outside the work and evaluating it: the speech that supposedly discredits the critic requires a republic of them. What it rules out is different — judging without a standard, and judging as a way of never having to act. See the other nine-tenths for where these passages sit in the argument.

1918: the duty to criticise a president

The strongest external evidence for his settled view is an editorial published eight years later, in the middle of a war he supported, attacking the president running it: “Sedition, a Free Press, and Personal Rule”, Kansas City Star, 7 May 1918, collected in Roosevelt in the Kansas City Star: War-Time Editorials (Houghton Mifflin, 1921), pp. 148–150. The wording below is checked against a proofread transcription of that printing.

“The President is merely the most important among a large number of public servants. He should be supported or opposed exactly to the degree which is warranted by his good conduct or bad conduct, his efficiency or inefficiency in rendering loyal, able, and disinterested service to the Nation as a whole. Therefore it is absolutely necessary that there should be full liberty to tell the truth about his acts, and this means that it is exactly necessary to blame him when he does wrong as to praise him when he does right. Any other attitude in an American citizen is both base and servile. To announce that there must be no criticism of the President, or that we are to stand by the President, right or wrong, is not only unpatriotic and servile, but is morally treasonable to the American public.”— Theodore Roosevelt, Kansas City Star, 7 May 1918

The same editorial asks Congress to “guarantee the right of the press and people to speak the truth freely of all their public servants, including the President, and to criticize them in the severest terms of truth whenever they come short in their public duty.” That is about as far as an American politician has gone in defending criticism — written by the author of “It is not the critic who counts”. The qualifier is the same one as in 1910: truth. Severity is licensed without limit; accuracy is not optional.

The context should be stated rather than smoothed over. In May 1918 Roosevelt held no office, had sons at the front in France, and was consumed by hostility to Woodrow Wilson. He was, at that moment, the man outside the arena pointing out how the strong man stumbled. The argument is self-serving. It is also correct, and it is on the record over his name.

1906: the muck-rake speech, which is not what it is remembered as

The other document in evidence is the address of 14 April 1906, at the laying of the corner stone of the House Office Building — the speech that put muckraker into English. It is remembered as an attack on investigative journalism. Read whole, it is a defence of it with a condition attached.

Roosevelt takes his figure from Bunyan: the man “who could look no way but downward, with the muck-rake in his hand”, who refuses the celestial crown offered him and keeps raking the filth of the floor. Everyone remembers the insult and forgets what follows it: “Now, it is very necessary that we should not flinch from seeing what is vile and debasing. There is filth on the floor, and it must be scraped up with the muck-rake; and there are times and places where this service is the most needed of all the services that can be performed.” The condemnation attaches to a specific person: “the man who never does anything else, who never thinks or speaks or writes, save of his feats with the muck-rake”. Again the qualifying clause carries the whole judgement.

“There should be relentless exposure of and attack upon every evil man whether politician or business man, every evil practice, whether in politics, in business, or in social life. I hail as a benefactor every writer or speaker, every man who, on the platform, or in book, magazine, or newspaper, with merciless severity makes such attack, provided always that he in his turn remembers that the attack is of use only if it is absolutely truthful.”— Theodore Roosevelt, “The Man with the Muck-Rake”, 14 April 1906

His objection is to indiscriminate attack, and the reason is instrumental rather than genteel: sweeping accusation debases the currency accurate accusation needs. “If the whole picture is painted black there remains no hue whereby to single out the rascals for distinction from their fellows. Such painting finally induces a kind of moral color-blindness”. The result, he argues, is that people “grow as suspicious of the accusation as of the offense” — which “gives hope to every knave, and is the despair of honest men”. Those who assault character indiscriminately, he adds, “invite a reaction which is sure to tell powerfully in favor of the unscrupulous scoundrel who really ought to be attacked, who ought to be exposed, who ought, if possible, to be put in the penitentiary.” The muckrakers, he concludes, “are often indispensable to the well-being of society”.

The tension should not be dissolved. In 1906 Roosevelt was president, the press was investigating his allies as well as his enemies, and he wanted the volume down. In 1918 he was out of power and wanted it up. The principle held steady — criticism must be truthful and must aim at repair — but his appetite for it tracked whether he was the target. That is a fact about the man rather than a refutation of the principle; the page on Roosevelt in 1910 has more of it.

The distinction he is actually drawing

Three cuts run through all three documents, and they are not the same cut.

  • Criticism that serves the work versus criticism performed instead of work. The test is what it is for. A note that makes the next version better sits on one side; a note whose function is to display the writer's discernment on the other. Both can be accurate. Only one has a purpose beyond its author.
  • The judge who has done the thing versus the judge whose only relation to the thing is judgement. Roosevelt's phrase is “work which the critic himself never tries to perform”. The claim is that never having attempted a thing distorts the estimate, because from outside the real constraints are invisible. It is his weakest claim and the most abused, but not empty: it is why the useful reviewer has usually shipped something.
  • The cynic, whose position cannot lose. The sharpest of the three, and the one the paragraph is actually about. The cynic risks nothing, predicts failure, is vindicated by every failure, and is insulated from every success, which he can call luck or compromise. Holding no standard — “good and evil are as one” — nothing counts as evidence against him. The flaw is not unkindness but unfalsifiability. A criticism that could never be wrong is not a criticism; it is a stance.

Where the passage fails, and the version that survives

The honest objection is that the second cut — only doers may judge — would destroy institutions a republic runs on. Whole professions exist to criticise from outside, and their value depends on being outside.

  • Auditors. An auditor who has run the company's books is disqualified, not qualified. Independence is the product.
  • Judges and juries. No one asks a juror whether he has committed the offence before letting him weigh the evidence.
  • Inspectors and regulators. The point of an air-accident investigator is that she did not build the aircraft and owes nothing to those who did.
  • Journalists. A reporter who has never held office is precisely who a public official should have to answer to.
  • Peer review and replication. Science advances by strangers trying to break work they did not do.
  • The loyal opposition. Its function is to criticise a government whose job it is not doing. Roosevelt spent his last years in exactly that position, and defended it in the Star.

Read literally, “It is not the critic who counts” guts all of that, and anyone quoting it to a regulator or a reviewer is making the literal claim. A defensible version is narrower: credit is not distributed by criticism. The sentence is about the ledger of honour, not the licence to speak. A critic can be entirely right about the work and still not be who the credit belongs to, because criticising a thing is not a way of having done it. Everything else the passage is asked to carry — that criticism is worthless, that outsiders should be silent, that only participants may object — is smuggled in. The critiques page takes the objections further.

The paragraph that gets quoted attacks a man who criticises what he will not attempt. The paragraph gets used to attack people who criticise things they are paid, elected, or licensed to criticise. Those are opposite operations, and the sentence Roosevelt wrote to separate them — about the man who sneers alike at criticism and performance — is the one nobody quotes.

A test, in four questions

The distinction is usable applied inward — as a check on one's own criticism, not a way of disqualifying someone else's. Four questions do most of the work, each anchored in the text.

  1. Is it true? The only condition Roosevelt attaches in all three documents. Severity is permitted; inaccuracy is not — “the attack is of use only if it is absolutely truthful”.
  2. What would change if it were accepted? Criticism aimed at repair names the change it wants; criticism aimed at status names only the fault.
  3. What would count as being wrong? If no outcome could falsify the position, it is the cynic's role — the easiest one available, by Roosevelt's own ranking.
  4. Would it be said to someone with the power to make it cost something? The 1918 test: criticism aimed only downward or sideways is a different act from criticism aimed at whoever is responsible.
