The other nine-tenths: wealth, the family, self-restraint, the nations

“Citizenship in a Republic” · Sorbonne, 23 April 1910 · page 05 of 13
Primary textAfrican and European Addresses, Scribner 1910 ↗
TL;DR — The arena paragraph is about four per cent of the speech. The rest argues that a republic lives or dies on the ordinary citizen's character: intellect ranked below self-restraint, wealth judged only by how it was got and used, no hatred permitted for either the rich or the poor, and nations held to the standard of honourable men. It also condemns voluntary childlessness as a crime and assigns the woman the household and the man the work and the rifle. The famous passage takes its meaning from all of that.

Quotation collections lift paragraph 10 out and throw the container away. That is why it travels, and why it can be made to mean almost anything. This page is the container: the other thirty-eight paragraphs, including the ones nobody prints on a poster and the one nobody prints at all.

Character before intellect — and cleverness as a hazard

The speech opens with a frame, not an exhortation. Under a monarchy or an oligarchy the quality of the rulers decides everything, and the ordinary citizen is, in Roosevelt's phrase, an almost negligible quantity (6). A republic reverses the arithmetic: success or failure turns on the way “the average man, the average woman, does his or her duty” (7). Hence the limit he draws — “The stream will not permanently rise higher than the main source” — and the corollary that leaders must stand well above the average to hold it there.

He then tells the Sorbonne that the trained intellects before him are the class most exposed to the failings he is about to name (8), chiefly the cynic's pose. Only then does he rank the goods.

“We must ever remember that no keenness and subtleness of intellect, no polish, no cleverness, in any way make up for the lack of the great solid qualities.”— Theodore Roosevelt, Citizenship in a Republic, paragraph 12

Read carelessly that is anti-intellectualism, and he guards against the reading in the same paragraph by paying “all homage to intellect” before demoting it. The harder claim arrives in paragraph 21: unguided by a moral sense, “the more efficient he is the worse he is”. Talent is a multiplier with a sign in front of it, and that sentence is the hinge of the address.

The qualities he actually names

Summaries of this speech often say Roosevelt prescribes honesty, courage and common sense. He never uses that formula. The word honesty does not appear in the address at all; the nearest he comes is “good faith and sense of honor”, inside his definition of character. What he does name is one list:

“Self-restraint, self-mastery, common-sense, the power of accepting individual responsibility and yet of acting in conjunction with others, courage and resolution—these are the qualities which mark a masterful people.”— Theodore Roosevelt, Citizenship in a Republic, paragraph 12

Note what leads. Not courage — self-restraint, twice over. A reader arriving from the arena paragraph expects daring at the head of the list and gets the ability to stop oneself. Paragraph 13 gives a blunter everyday triad: “the will and the power to work, to fight at need, and to have plenty of healthy children”. And 29 supplies the method he wants applied to public questions — put every proposal through “hard-headed examination”, judge it on merits, disregard who proposed it, try it if it looks good and drop it if it proves bad. That is the least quoted useful thing in the speech.

Money, greed, and the duty of the rich man

Roosevelt comes at wealth from both directions, which is what makes it hard to weaponise. First the duty to provide: the average man must earn his living and should feel contemptible if he does not, “at whichever end of the social scale he stands” (13). “He must pull his own weight first” (15), and the man whose enthusiasm for humanity in the abstract leaves him unable to keep his own family earns contempt, not admiration.

Then the reversal. Paragraph 16 refuses the rich man any credit for being rich: “I decline to recognize the mere multi-millionaire, the man of mere wealth, as an asset of value to any country”. Great business intelligences deserve ample reward — but the man who, far past providing for his own, piles up a fortune “for the acquisition or retention of which he returns no corresponding benefit to the nation as a whole” should be made to feel an unworthy citizen, neither admired nor envied. His summary is one sentence, and in 1910 it was a live platform rather than a pleasantry:

“In every civilized society property rights must be carefully safeguarded; ordinarily, and in the great majority of cases, human rights and property rights are fundamentally and in the long run identical; but when it clearly appears that there is a real conflict between them, human rights must have the upper hand, for property belongs to man and not man to property.”— Theodore Roosevelt, Citizenship in a Republic, paragraph 17

Two other admired gifts get the same instrument-not-virtue audit (18–19): money-making and oratory are judged solely by the use made of them, and eloquence that persuades people to put false values on things is “a power for mischief”. The journalist, he adds, is the orator's more influential brother and gets the same treatment.

The home, the mother, and the birth-rate argument

This is the part essentially never quoted, and it is not a stray remark. It is the climax of his list of ordinary virtues. Having ranked work and readiness to fight, he says the third matters more than either.

“The greatest of all curses is the curse of sterility, and the severest of all condemnations should be that visited upon wilful sterility. The first essential in any civilization is that the man and the woman shall be father and mother of healthy children, so that the race shall increase and not decrease.”— Theodore Roosevelt, Citizenship in a Republic, paragraph 15

He presses further in the same paragraph. Childlessness by choice is not a misfortune but “one of those crimes of ease and self-indulgence, of shrinking from pain and effort and risk, which in the long run Nature punishes more heavily than any other”. Nothing offsets it: no refinement, no material progress, no development of art and literature “can in any way compensate for the loss of the great fundamental virtues; and of these great fundamental virtues, the greatest is the race's power to perpetuate the race”. A people that will not reproduce, he tells a room of French academics, may as well stop boasting about anything else it has done.

The division of labour is stated once, without qualification:

“The homely virtues of the household, the ordinary workaday virtues which make the woman a good housewife and house-mother, which make the man a hard worker, a good husband and father, a good soldier at need, stand at the bottom of character.”— Theodore Roosevelt, Citizenship in a Republic, paragraph 22

The modern objection has three parts. The roles are assigned by sex rather than offered: the household to the woman, earning and soldiering to the man. The speech contains no picture of a woman acting as a citizen outside the home — the one place it grants her equal standing is “the average man, the average woman, does his or her duty” in paragraph 7, and that is never developed. And a private reproductive decision is being classed as a crime against the nation by a recent head of state, with public contempt named as the enforcement.

Then the vocabulary. Roosevelt does not use the phrase race suicide in this speech — it is absent from the 1910 printed text, and any source saying otherwise is quoting something else. But he was the term's most famous exponent. It was coined by the American sociologist Edward A. Ross around 1900, and Roosevelt used it repeatedly before the Sorbonne, including in his March 1905 address to the National Congress of Mothers, where he said a race which practised race suicide would thereby show itself unfit to exist. In the American politics of that decade the phrase carried an explicit demographic anxiety — that native-born white Americans were being outbred by immigrants — and it fed directly into the eugenic and immigration-restriction arguments of the next two decades.

Two things have to be held at once. What the Sorbonne text itself says is a natalist argument about duty, framed for two republics alike. What the record around it supplies is the freight that argument carried when Roosevelt made it elsewhere in explicitly demographic terms. Restoring the context of the arena paragraph means inheriting this paragraph too.

Against the reactionary and the revolutionary at once

Roosevelt refuses both ends. “We can just as little afford to follow the doctrinaires of an extreme individualism as the doctrinaires of an extreme socialism,” he says (24), then declares himself “a strong individualist by personal habit, inheritance, and conviction” before conceding that as society grows denser, things once left to individuals are better done together. His example is deliberately unglamorous: isolated farms each mind their own drainage and water supply, a town cannot, and only experiment fixes the line. He wants to “turn the tool user more and more into the tool owner”, and will adopt any measure proposed by Socialists that happens to be good while rejecting the system whole.

The symmetry is sharpest in paragraph 30:

“Remember always that the same measure of condemnation should be extended to the arrogance which would look down upon or crush any man because he is poor, and to the envy and hatred which would destroy a man because he is wealthy.”— Theodore Roosevelt, Citizenship in a Republic, paragraph 30

He calls these two sides of the same shield: the heir who ruins his weaker neighbours and the demagogue who excites the propertyless to plunder are the same man at root. Then the historical claim the passage exists to support. Republics have fallen before, and the prime factor was parties dividing along the line separating wealth from poverty — and it made no difference which side won, oligarchy or mob; once loyalty to a class had replaced loyalty to the republic the end was already there. His preferred geometry: the cleavage between good and bad citizenship “runs at right angles to, and not parallel with, the lines of cleavage between class and class”. Paragraph 31 adds the temperament required — intensity of conviction plus broad tolerance of difference of conviction.

Self-government requires self-restraint

Free institutions have no external support. Nothing holds them up but the private character of the people living under them, which is why paragraph 21 ends as a warning about the electorate rather than about politicians: a public that judges men by success alone, condoning wickedness because the wicked man triumphs, fails to grasp that “in the last analysis free institutions rest upon the character of citizenship”, and so proves itself unfit for liberty.

He wants both halves (20) — the qualities that make for efficiency and the qualities that direct it to public good, neither availing without the other. He has no patience for harmlessness, since “There is little place in active life for the timid good man”, and lands on a flat formula: “The good citizen is not a good citizen unless he is an efficient citizen.” The next paragraph clamps the moral sense back on.

Two failure modes get named. The doctrinaire: the closet philosopher explaining from his library how men ought to be governed under ideal conditions is useless, and the impracticable visionary is more often the embittered foe of the real reformer than his precursor (22–23) — a charge Roosevelt turns on any preacher of ideals who will not try to live them. And the demagogue, where 32 gives the cleanest test in the speech: beware the man who asks for your support on the ground that he is hostile to other citizens, or will get you something at their expense. He illustrates it with a ranching anecdote — a new cowhand cheerfully burning the boss's brand onto a neighbour's unbranded calf, fired on the spot with “if you will steal for me you will steal from me.” A public man who offers to do wrong in your interest will, when it pays, do wrong against it.

Justice, equality, and the limits he puts on it

The speech says less about law than about equality, and what it says is bounded. Paragraph 25 begins with a refusal to pretend — “We should not say that men are equal where they are not equal” — and in the same breath commits him to a measurable equality, at minimum by preventing the inequality due to force or fraud. He then hands the definition to Lincoln, quoting a passage on the Declaration of Independence: men were declared equal in certain inalienable rights, a standard maxim to be constantly looked to and constantly approximated even if never perfectly attained.

From there he draws a hard line between opportunity and outcome (27). There should so far as possible be equality of opportunity to render service; but while service is unequal, reward must be unequal, since paying the incompetent as the capable creates “a new kind of privilege, the privilege of folly and weakness” — and “special privilege is injustice, whatever form it takes”. Paragraph 28 compresses it: “Let us try to level up, but let us beware of the evil of levelling down.”

The most durable line here is a test rather than a doctrine: “Probably the best test of true love of liberty in any country is the way in which minorities are treated in that country” (30). What the speech never asks is why opportunity is unequal to begin with. His answers — shifting burdens so they are more equitably borne, turning the tool user into the tool owner — are gestures rather than mechanisms.

What one nation owes another, and the tribute to France

The last movement (34–37) extends the argument outward: the state is the aggregation of individuals, so its duties to other states run on the same moral terms. He opens by disowning “a foolish cosmopolitanism” — a man must be a good patriot first, and whoever claims his love of mankind has dissolved his love of country usually proves an undesirable citizen of wherever he is standing. The test is domestic: distrust the man who views his own country and all others with the same tepid indifference, as you would one who took an equally dispassionate view of his wife and his mother.

The patriotism he wants is bounded by honour (35). The true patriot sees that the nation “neither inflicts nor suffers wrong”, and Roosevelt denies any separate political morality — a nation may not treat other nations in a spirit an honourable man would not use with other men. Paragraph 36 concedes the practical asymmetry with unusual precision: the capital difference between municipal and international law is that “there is a sanction for the one and no sanction for the other”. He expects international law to grow enforceable over generations and calls it the duty of statesmen to build substitutes for force — then refuses to disarm meanwhile, since if peace and justice conflict “we scorn the man who would not stand for justice though the whole world came in arms against him”. He was speaking in Paris in April 1910; four years later that stopped being theoretical for everyone in the room.

The parting tribute doubles as the speech's own counter-example. France and the United States are the only two republics among the great powers; a calamity to one would be a sorrow to the other; “For her to sink would be a loss to all the world.” He reaches for Froissart on a time of dire disaster, when the realm of France was never so stricken that men were not left who would valiantly fight for it. And earlier, in paragraph 11, he had used France to concede the point his suspicion of cultivation most needs — that a high artistic and literary development is compatible with notable leadership in arms and statecraft.

Reading the arena paragraph again

Put back in the argument, the famous sentence changes weight in three ways.

The qualifier stops being decorative. Roosevelt's man “spends himself in a worthy cause”, and the eight thousand words around him are largely an attempt to say what makes a cause worthy: service actually rendered, wealth judged by its use, family kept, the republic above one's class, other nations not wronged, the moral sense clamped over the efficiency. Remove the qualifier and the passage praises exertion as such — which paragraph 21 explicitly refuses.

The register is duty, not self-actualisation. Nothing in the speech promises that entering the arena will make anyone feel alive, or whole, or authentic. The claim is that a republic dies unless ordinary people do ordinary duties, and the man who will not act because he might be criticised is failing one. The modern reading — be brave, risk being seen, the critics do not matter — is a psychological doctrine grafted onto a civic one. Not absurd, and the passage takes the graft more easily than most; still a graft.

And restoring the context does not tidy the speech up. It supplies the birth-rate paragraph, the housewife sentence, and a default citizen who is male throughout. Arguing that the quotation should be read in context commits a reader to that context, including the parts that are not defensible.

Carry this away: the arena paragraph is a conclusion, and the speech is the argument it concludes. The single most useful correction the rest supplies is paragraph 21 — efficiency without a moral sense makes a man worse, not merely useless. Daring greatly in an unworthy cause is condemned by the same speech that supplies the phrase.
