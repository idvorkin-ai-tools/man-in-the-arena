Mandela and Pienaar: checking the story

“Citizenship in a Republic” · Sorbonne, 23 April 1910 · page 10 of 13
Primary artifactAnthony Peckham, Invictus (aka The Human Factor), second draft, 22 May 2007 ↗
TL;DR — The story that Nelson Mandela handed François Pienaar Roosevelt's arena passage before the 1995 Rugby World Cup final is repeated in hundreds of places and documented in none of them. The film Invictus uses W. E. Henley's poem, not Roosevelt. Neither John Carlin's book, which the film is based on, nor Pienaar's own autobiography contains the word “Roosevelt” in any relevant sense. Every version of the Roosevelt claim traces back to a single unsourced clause in a British newspaper column published seven weeks after the film opened. The Mandela–Henley connection, by contrast, is genuinely well sourced.

Most of this map is about what Roosevelt said and what it means. This page is about how to check a story — because the single most-repeated fact about the arena passage's afterlife appears, on inspection, to be a rumour with a paper trail. The trail is short enough to walk end to end, and walking it is more useful than any general warning about misinformation.

The claim, as it circulates

In its standard form: before the 1995 Rugby World Cup, Mandela gave the Springbok captain François Pienaar a copy of Roosevelt's “man in the arena” passage; South Africa beat New Zealand in the final; Clint Eastwood's Invictus swapped in a poem for cinematic reasons. English Wikipedia carries it on two separate articles. The Theodore Roosevelt Presidential Library's page on the speech makes a related claim about Mandela and the passage, uncited. Motivational sites, leadership blogs, speaker bureaus and at least four trade books published since 2012 repeat it.

It has everything a good transmission story needs. A revered figure. A decisive afternoon. A text about entering the arena, handed to a man about to enter one. The only thing missing is a source.

What the film actually does

Invictus (2009), directed by Eastwood from Anthony Peckham's screenplay, does not use Roosevelt at all. It uses W. E. Henley's sixteen-line poem “Invictus” (1875) — which is where the film gets its title. Peckham's draft is explicit. Mandela, alone in his office after a television pundit writes off the Springboks, takes a sheet of his personal letterhead and, the stage direction says, “we see him write, and then underline, the title INVICTUS by W.E. Henley”, then writes the first line from memory. He hands the envelope to Pienaar on a training pitch: “This helped me, many times. Perhaps it will help you, too.” Pienaar's partner finds it on the desk of his hotel room and asks what it is. “A poem. From the President.”

Two details matter. First, in the script the poem is sent before the tournament's opening match against Australia, not before the final. Second, this draft is dated 22 May 2007 — more than a year before Carlin's book was published, and titled after the book's British title, The Human Factor. The Henley device was in the screenplay before the source book existed in print.

“On Robben Island, when things were very hard, I found inspiration in a poem. … A Victorian poem. Just words. But they helped me to stand when all I wanted was to lie down.”— the Mandela character, Peckham's Invictus screenplay, second draft, 2007

What Carlin's book says

The film is adapted from John Carlin's Playing the Enemy: Nelson Mandela and the Game That Made a Nation (2008), reissued in the United States as Invictus after the film. Carlin interviewed Pienaar, Mandela and much of the surrounding cast; if a president handed a rugby captain a nineteenth-century American speech, this is the book it would be in.

It is not. A full-text search of the scanned editions on the Internet Archive returns exactly one occurrence of “Roosevelt” in the book, in a list of leaders said to have possessed the common touch — Lincoln, Roosevelt, Churchill, de Gaulle, Kennedy. The phrase “man in the arena” does not appear. Nor does “Theodore Roosevelt”. The Henley poem does appear, but in the front matter of the retitled Invictus edition, printed as an epigraph after the film made it the selling point; the scans of the original Playing the Enemy printing do not contain the poem's text.

This was noticed early. In June 2010, in the comments under a British public-speaking blog that had asserted the Roosevelt version, a reader reported having “read through… Carlin's book (now published as Invictus) without finding mention of the gift at all”, and asked where the claim came from. The blog's author replied that Anthony Sampson's authorised Mandela biography documents the Henley poem but “doesn't refer to him giving a copy to François Pienaar”, and guessed that the source must be Pienaar himself — “perhaps a copy of his autobiography, ‘Rainbow Warrior’ might be a source?” Nobody in that exchange could name one.

What Pienaar has said

Pienaar has told the story of 1995 for thirty years, in interviews, documentaries and paid keynotes. The recurring detail is not a document. It is a jersey: Mandela walking into the changing room wearing a Springbok shirt with the captain's number six on the back. “It was just insane seeing Mr Mandela walking into our changing room before the final wearing a Springbok on his heart,” he told World Rugby. In a CNN feature published the week Invictus opened — an interview conducted with both Pienaar and Carlin, at the exact moment when “and what he really gave me was Roosevelt” would have been the obvious thing to say — neither man mentions any text at all. Pienaar's own 1999 autobiography, Rainbow Warrior, is scanned and full-text searchable, and does not contain the word “Roosevelt”.

Absence of evidence in one memoir proves little. Absence across the memoir, the biographer's book, the journalist's book and the contemporaneous interviews starts to mean something.

Where the Roosevelt version came from

The trail has a first step, and it is datable. On 19 December 2009 — eight days after Invictus opened in American cinemas — an editor added a single sentence to English Wikipedia's article on “Citizenship in a Republic”: Nelson Mandela also gave a copy of this speech to Francois Pienaar, captain of the South African rugby team, before the start of the 1995 Rugby World Cup. The edit summary reads “add paragraph from ‘invictus’ wikipedia page”. No citation was attached.

A citation arrived later, and it is the only one either Wikipedia article has ever carried: a Dominic Sandbrook column in the Daily Telegraph of 30 January 2010, about the failings of contemporary British politicians. The relevant text is one clause in a paragraph about the film, which had not yet opened in Britain:

“In the film, Mandela gives Pienaar a poem by William Ernest Henley, Invictus, that inspires him to glory; in reality, he gave him an extract from a speech by Theodore Roosevelt.”— Dominic Sandbrook, “British leaders: they're not what they were”, Daily Telegraph, 30 January 2010

That is the whole of it. Sandbrook names no speech, no occasion and no source; the words “arena” and “1995” do not appear in his column, and the claim is an aside inside an argument about Gordon Brown. Wikipedia's current text then adds what Sandbrook did not say — that it was “a text of ‘The Man in the Arena’ passage”, delivered “at the actual event” — and from there the specificity spreads outward. By 2012 it is in books. By now it is in the furniture.

Mandela and Henley: the part that holds up

The contrast is instructive, because one half of the story is properly sourced. Anthony Sampson's Mandela: The Authorised Biography (1999), written with Mandela's cooperation ten years before the film, treats “Invictus” as his poem — describing a later line of Mandela's as an echo of “his favourite poem on Robben Island: ‘I am the master of my fate’”. Sampson's endnotes for that material cite Henley's 1875 text and, crucially, “Mandela to Richard Stengel”: the interview tapes Stengel recorded while working on Long Walk to Freedom. Mandela's own remarks about the poem, from a conversation with Stengel dated to around March 1993, are printed in the authorised compilation Notes to the Future (2012).

That is what a documented transmission looks like: a named informant, a dated interview, a biographer's citation, all predating the film that made the story famous. Even here, the softer versions should be resisted — that Mandela “kept the poem on a scrap of paper in his cell”, or recited it to particular prisoners on particular days, is usually stated more confidently than the record supports. But the core claim — that Henley's poem mattered to Mandela in prison, on his own testimony — is solid.

Mandela and Roosevelt: what turns up

Searching specifically for Mandela quoting or invoking the arena passage — in his speeches, his autobiography, the authorised quotation collections, or the biographies — turns up nothing. Not a hedged instance, not a paraphrase: nothing. The passage's documented South African life runs the other way; Gordon Brown used it as an epigraph in a chapter of Courage: Eight Portraits (2007), a book that also contains a chapter on Mandela, but Brown never connects the two, and that coincidence of proximity is the closest thing to a pre-film link this search found.

The verdict. Documented: the film uses Henley's “Invictus”, deliberately and from an early draft; Mandela's attachment to that poem, on his own testimony to Richard Stengel, recorded by Anthony Sampson in 1999; Mandela's Springbok jersey and the trophy handed to Pienaar at Ellis Park on 24 June 1995. Film: the handwritten letterhead, the envelope on the training pitch, the recitation in the Robben Island cell. Folklore: the Roosevelt version. On the present evidence, Mandela giving Pienaar the arena passage is a post-2009 conflation — a correction invented to fix a fictional scene, which then acquired the authority of a correction. If a genuine 1994–95 source exists, it has not surfaced in a book, an interview or an archive in thirty years, and everyone repeating the claim is citing the same 2010 newspaper aside without knowing it.

How a good story hardens into a fact

The mechanism here is worth naming, because it is reusable. A film dramatises, and does so honestly — nobody involved in Invictus claimed the letterhead scene was reportage. A commentator, writing quickly about the film, reaches for a corrective flourish: in reality, he gave him. The corrective form is what does the damage, because a correction sounds better sourced than the thing it corrects. An encyclopedia picks the clause up and supplies the missing specifics from context. A listicle copies the encyclopedia. A trade book cites the listicle, and now the claim has a footnote. Nothing in the chain is a lie, and no link in it ever went back to 1995.

Note also that the versions circulating usually quote a variant text of the passage — “who errs, who comes short”, “who neither know victory nor defeat” — rather than the 1910 Scribner wording. The people correcting the film have not checked Roosevelt either. That is the same failure at a smaller scale, and this map's close reading lists the differences.

There is a reason this passage in particular attracts stories like this. It is a text about the difference between watching and entering, delivered in the second person of the imagination — and the natural narrative form of a text about entering is somebody handing it to somebody at the door. The arena passage has collected such stories for a century: its afterlife includes several claims about who carried it, who kept it in a wallet, and who read it before the decisive moment, most of which thin out under the same treatment applied here. A quotation that flatters the person who acts will always attract legends about being given to people at the moment of acting. The paragraph itself, read in its place in the speech, needs none of them.
